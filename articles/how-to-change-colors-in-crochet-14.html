How to Change Colors in Crochet

Switch colors at the final pull-through of a stitch so the new shade forms the top of the next stitch.

A clean color change is often prepared before the old-color stitch is fully finished. The unused strand should not pull the fabric inward.

Before you begin

Keep both colors accessible and check whether the pattern says to carry the unused yarn or fasten it off. Long carries can show or affect stretch.

Step-by-step instructions

  1. Work the stitch before the change until its final yarn-over and pull-through remain.
  2. Drop the old color and use the new color for that final pull-through; the new shade reaches the stitch top.
  3. Continue in the new color, keeping the join snug but not puckered.
  4. For a short span, use the pattern’s carry method; for a longer span, fastening off and rejoining may avoid a loose float.

What to check

Change at the stitch top

Inspect both sides. The boundary should sit at the intended stitch, and any carried strand should not draw the fabric inward.

Frequently asked questions

Should I cut the old yarn every time?

Not necessarily. The pattern may ask you to carry it, float it, or fasten off. Distance and fabric matter.

Why does the old color peek through?

The change may have happened after the stitch was complete. Try using the new shade for the final pull-through.

Plan where the color change belongs

For changing colors, use this as the starting rule: complete the final pull-through of the stitch with the new color when the pattern calls for a clean transition. The stitch sequence and the pattern’s edge rules determine where the hook goes and what counts. Read the relevant instructions before beginning and keep them nearby as you work. If a general tutorial and the pattern give different directions, follow the pattern’s stated convention; another project may use a different method.

Change color on the stated pull-through

Before proceeding, confirm where the color change falls relative to a stitch, row, or round. Write any project-specific choice in your notes so you can apply it consistently. If the pattern or label leaves the detail unclear, look for its own key or maker guidance rather than assuming that another project follows the same convention. This short check can prevent a small misunderstanding from affecting the rest of the work.

Keep the transition at the right boundary

A practical way to apply changing colors: Work a short swatch and practice at a low-visibility edge before changing in a motif. After a few stitches, pause and look at the loops before repeating the movement. Good light and smooth yarn can make the structure easier to see while you learn. If the sample does not match the pattern’s directions, undo the short section and repeat the step slowly; changing one movement at a time makes the cause easier to spot.

Choose whether to carry or cut the yarn

A common mistake in changing colors is waiting until after the stitch is complete and leaving a color jog. Watch for this sign: the transition lands one stitch later than intended. That can change the stitch count or leave a loop difficult to work into. Compare the recent work with the pattern and locate the first point where they differ. Correct that detail before continuing; stretching the fabric or adding an unplanned step can hide the cause without fixing it.

Check the back of the color change

If the transition lands one stitch later than intended, first check where the color change falls relative to a stitch, row, or round. Then compare the stitch sequence, insertion point, and edge count at the last correct row with what you actually have. Change one thing at a time so you can tell whether it addresses the problem. If the instructions and materials match but the result still differs, use the project’s gauge, sizing, substitution, or care notes as relevant; those are more useful than a universal fix.

Correct a jog before continuing

Remember: plan the change at the stated stitch boundary. Following the same insertion and pull-through sequence helps keep the stitch structure recognizable from one row to the next. If you choose an alternative, record it and compare a small sample or written step before applying the change across the project.

Practice a clean color transition

To make changing colors easier to repeat, use this sequence: practice at a low-visibility edge before changing in a motif, inspect the result, and only then continue. At first, slow down at the point where the pattern changes direction, begins a new round, or starts a repeat. A removable marker or brief row note can hold your place while you learn the construction. Once the sequence is clear, you can work more steadily without guessing which loop or instruction comes next.

Conclusion: Changing colors

Keep this lesson in mind: plan the change at the stated stitch boundary. Carry the pattern’s stitch and edge rule into the next row. For a clean transition, many patterns change color on the final pull-through of the preceding stitch. Follow the project’s exact color-change note.
