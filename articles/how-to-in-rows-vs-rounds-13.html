How to Crochet in Rows vs. Rounds

Understand whether a project is worked back and forth in rows or continuously around in rounds.

By Ariane Moreira

Rows turn at an edge; rounds continue around a center or tube. Construction affects the next stitch location, turning, and seam visibility.

Before you begin

Look for pattern cues such as “turn,” “join,” or “round.” A project can combine rows and rounds, so follow each instruction rather than judging by shape.

Step-by-step instructions

  1. If the instruction says to work across and turn, finish the row, rotate the piece, and begin as directed.
  2. If it says to join, close the round at the stated point before moving on.
  3. If it says to continue in a spiral, keep working around and move your marker to the new round start.
  4. Check whether the front stays facing you or alternates; this can affect texture and color placement.

What to check

Follow the construction verbs

Rows create side edges and turning-chain transitions. Rounds form a continuous path, though joined rounds may show a seam. Use the written instruction to settle ambiguity.

Frequently asked questions

Can a blanket be made in rounds?

Yes. Shape alone does not determine construction; the pattern says whether to use rows or rounds.

Do I turn after a joined round?

Only if the pattern says so. Some designs turn to alternate seam direction; others do not.

How can I tell where a round starts?

A joined round begins at the pattern’s join; a spiral needs a marker because it has no joining seam. Follow the round-start note.

Can one project use both rows and rounds?

Yes. A project may use rows for a flat section and rounds for a border or tube. Follow each instruction rather than assuming the whole project uses one construction.

Read construction verbs as instructions

“Turn” usually means finish the row and rotate the piece. “Join” marks a place to close a round, while “continue” or “work in a spiral” means keep going around without a join. These words affect where the next stitch goes, so read them before moving the hook. A piece can look circular while still using joined rounds, or look flat while a pattern builds it in rounds. Shape alone does not settle the construction.

Consider the seam and fabric orientation

Rows create side edges and may alternate which side faces you. Joined rounds can create a seam; continuous spirals avoid a joining seam but can make the round boundary harder to see. Some patterns turn after a joined round to change seam direction or keep texture consistent. Those choices are design details, not errors. If the front and back of a stitch look different, note which side the pattern expects to face outward before you continue.

Notice which side of the fabric faces outward

When rows are turned, the right and wrong sides may alternate unless the pattern tells you to turn in a way that keeps one side facing. Joined rounds can also be turned, while a spiral usually progresses continuously. This can affect textured stitches, color placement, and how the finished fabric looks. Mark the right side if the pattern distinguishes it, and do not infer the front from the project’s shape alone.

Read a transition before changing construction

A design can move from rows to rounds or use both within one piece. At the transition, check whether the next instruction asks you to join, turn, or work around an edge. Count the stitches available along that edge and follow any corner or shaping directions. A row count and a round count describe different parts of the construction; mark them separately in written notes so you do not lose your place.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

  • Turning after every round without an instruction. This can change which side faces out or shift the seam. Check whether the pattern says to turn.
  • Joining a spiral at the marker. That creates a seam where the pattern may expect a continuous path. Follow the round-start note.
  • Assuming the project shape tells you the method. Read “turn,” “join,” and “spiral” in the instructions; shape alone is not enough.

Tips that make it easier

  • Circle construction verbs in the pattern before starting a new section.
  • Mark the round start in a spiral and move the marker as you go.
  • Check whether each row or round ends with the count the pattern gives.

A useful next step

Check the pattern’s instructions against a short sample before continuing, then follow its stated stitch and edge rules.
