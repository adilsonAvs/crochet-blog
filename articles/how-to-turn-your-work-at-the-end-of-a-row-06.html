How to Turn Your Work at the End of a Crochet Row

Turn a row of crochet without twisting the fabric or losing track of the edge stitches.

By Ariane Moreira

Turning changes the direction you work across a flat piece. The turning chain’s height and whether it counts as a stitch depend on the pattern.

Before you begin

Finish the row and check the pattern’s turning-chain count. Keep the hook loop secure as you rotate the work.

Step-by-step instructions

  1. After the final stitch, stop before pulling the hook loop larger.
  2. Rotate the piece so the working edge is positioned for the next row; do not rotate the hook through the fabric.
  3. Make the turning chain specified by the pattern. A US single-crochet row may use one chain, but this is not universal.
  4. Work into the first stitch or skip it only as directed, then check the row count.

What to check

Keep the row edge in view

The fabric should lie flat rather than twist. If the edge gains or loses stitches, check whether the turning chain counts and locate the first stitch before continuing.

Frequently asked questions

Which direction should I turn?

Use the same turning direction throughout the piece. Rotate the work rather than flipping the hook through a stitch.

Do I always chain one to turn?

No. The turning-chain height depends on the next stitch and pattern instructions.

Should I turn clockwise or counterclockwise?

Follow the direction shown in your pattern or tutorial and keep the fabric oriented consistently. Do not twist the hook through the active loop.

What the turn changes—and what it does not

Turning changes which edge is next to your hook; it does not add a stitch by itself. The turning chain, when the pattern calls for one, establishes height for the next row. Whether that chain is counted as a stitch is a separate instruction. Keep those two decisions separate: make the specified number of chains, then follow the pattern for where row one begins and where its final stitch belongs.

Locate the new row’s starting point

After rotating the fabric, look at the first stitch beside the turning chain. In a pattern where the chain does not count, that first stitch is usually worked into the first stitch of the previous row. If the chain does count, it may replace that edge stitch, depending on the instructions. A marker can identify the stitch you should use. At the far edge, use the same written rule to decide whether to work into the top of the turning chain. Check the total after the row so the two edges follow one consistent count.

Match the turning chain to the next stitch

A turning chain gives the next row room to reach its stitch height. A pattern may use one chain before a US single-crochet row, two before a half-double row, or more for taller stitches, but these are common conventions rather than a universal rule. Follow the pattern’s stated count and check whether it treats the chain as a stitch. Too few chains can pull the edge down; extra chains can leave a gap or add height. If the edge looks uneven, compare the turning chain and the first stitch before changing your hook or tension. Use the same edge rule before continuing across the new row.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

  • Turning before the last stitch is complete. This can leave a short row or an unfinished edge loop. Check the pattern’s final stitch before rotating the fabric.
  • Always chaining one. The turning-chain height depends on the next stitch and the pattern. Make the stated number of chains.
  • Working into the turning chain automatically. Some patterns count it as a stitch and others do not. Check the edge instruction before inserting the hook.

Tips that make it easier

  • Keep the active loop on the hook while you rotate the fabric.
  • Locate the next row’s first stitch before making the turning chain.
  • Place a removable marker in the edge stitch while practicing.

A practical next step

Keep the active loop on the hook while you rotate the fabric. Then check your result against the pattern before continuing.
