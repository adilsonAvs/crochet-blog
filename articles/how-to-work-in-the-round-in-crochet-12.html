How to Work in the Round in Crochet

Choose between joined rounds and continuous spirals, then mark the round start so your stitch placement stays organized.

Crochet in the round can build a tube or flat circle. The pattern tells you whether to join each round or continue without a join.

Before you begin

Use a stitch marker and read the joining instructions. Joined rounds have a visible seam; a spiral avoids that seam but makes the start harder to spot.

Step-by-step instructions

  1. Make the starting ring using the method specified, such as a chain loop or adjustable ring.
  2. Work the first round into the ring and mark the first stitch or round start.
  3. For joined rounds, close only where instructed, then make any starting chain stated for the next round.
  4. For continuous rounds, move the marker as you work; do not add a joining slip stitch unless directed.

What to check

The join changes the fabric

If the seam drifts or the shape becomes uneven, check whether the pattern calls for a spiral or joined rounds. Keep your chosen method consistent.

Frequently asked questions

Should every round be joined?

No. Some patterns use joined rounds and others use continuous spirals. Follow the stated construction.

Why use a stitch marker?

It identifies the round start, which is easy to lose in a spiral and useful when checking increases.

How do I keep a flat circle from curling?

Check the pattern’s increase count, stitch tension, and hook size. A circle can curl if its rounds do not add enough width for the chosen stitch and yarn.

Can I change from a spiral to joined rounds halfway through?

Only if the pattern allows the change. The transition can alter the seam, round count, and stitch placement, so check the instructions before switching.

Choose the starting ring the pattern describes

A project may begin with a chain loop, an adjustable ring, or another foundation. These starts are not interchangeable in every design: a ring that can be tightened may close a center more fully, while a chain loop has a fixed opening. Follow the pattern’s method and work the stated number of stitches into the center. Pull or secure the starting tail only as the method directs, then check that the first round lies flat.

Track joined rounds and spirals differently

In joined rounds, the pattern tells you where to close the round and how to begin the next one. The join can form a visible seam, and a starting chain may or may not count as a stitch. In a spiral, continue into the next stitch without joining and move a marker to show the new round start. Count at the marker and at any increase points. Switching methods mid-project can shift the boundary, so keep the same construction unless the pattern instructs otherwise.

Place increases where each round instructs

Circular patterns distribute increases to keep the fabric expanding. The location and number depend on the stitch, yarn, and design; do not move increases just to make a round easier to count. Mark the beginning of the round and use the written repeat to track each increase. If the piece ripples or cups, compare the stitch total with the pattern before changing the hook or adding unplanned stitches.

Check the center and outer edge as the circle grows

A starting ring may need to close fully, but pulling the tail too hard can distort the first stitches. After a few rounds, lay the piece flat and check that the center is secure and the outer edge is not pulled into a bowl. Compare the round count, increases, and gauge with the pattern. Different fibers and tensions can change how the circle sits, so use the pattern’s dimensions and swatch guidance when available.

Read increase placement before shaping

A flat circle usually grows by adding stitches in planned locations, while a tube may keep the same stitch count for several rounds. The pattern’s increase schedule creates the intended shape. Moving increases closer together can make a circle ripple; missing them can cause it to cup. Use the stated stitch count as your first check, then look at the fabric lying flat. If the shape still differs, compare gauge and stitch type with the pattern before changing the number or location of increases.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

  • Joining a spiral round by habit. This creates an unintended seam. Follow the pattern’s round method and move the marker instead when working continuously.
  • Forgetting to move the marker. The round start drifts and increases become harder to track. Move it into the first stitch of each new spiral round.
  • Counting the joining slip stitch automatically. Patterns differ on whether it counts. Use the stated count and starting-chain rule.

Tips that make it easier

  • Use a marker with a contrasting color so the round start is easy to spot.
  • Check the stitch total after each round that includes increases.
  • If a center ring remains open, check the starting method and tail before adding extra stitches.

A useful next step

Check the pattern’s instructions against a short sample before continuing, then follow its stated stitch and edge rules.
