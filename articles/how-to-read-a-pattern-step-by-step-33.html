How to Read a Crochet Pattern Step by Step

Read a crochet pattern by checking terminology, stitch key, repeat punctuation, and row counts before starting.

By Ariane Moreira

Patterns use conventions that may differ by publisher. Reading notes first can prevent a US/UK terminology mix-up or a misunderstood repeat.

Before you begin

Gather the listed yarn and hook, identify terminology and abbreviations, and mark the row or round you are working.

Step-by-step instructions

  1. Read materials, gauge, notes, and abbreviations before beginning.
  2. Scan the full instruction to locate repeats and any stated stitch count at a row’s end.
  3. Work one line at a time, marking repeat boundaries and checking counts before moving on.
  4. For unclear directions, consult the pattern key or publisher instead of importing a rule from another pattern.

What to check

Read the notes before the stitches

A row count helps check progress but does not explain every construction choice. If it differs, review turning-chain rules and repeat boundaries before undoing the row.

Frequently asked questions

Do parentheses always mean a repeat?

No. Patterns use punctuation differently. Read the key and exact wording around the marks.

What if an abbreviation is missing?

Check the publisher’s notes or contact the source. Uncommon terms may not have a universal meaning.

Should I read the whole pattern first?

Yes. The notes may define terminology, repeats, gauge, or construction choices that affect every later row.

Turn a line of pattern into actions

Read the complete line once before crocheting. Circle the stitch names, underline the repeat, and note the number of times it is worked. For example, “sc in next 3 sts, inc in next st” means four placements: one single crochet in each of three stitches, followed by two single crochets in one stitch. The exact abbreviation and punctuation still come from the pattern key.

If a line has brackets within parentheses, do not flatten the marks into one instruction. Work the inner group as written, then repeat the full group only the stated number of times. A row-end stitch count is a useful check, but it may count stitches differently from chains or spaces.

Read shaping and construction notes with the row

A pattern can tell you to increase at one edge, change colors, turn, or join a round. These actions affect what the next row means, so mark them before starting. When a pattern says “each” or “every,” identify the exact stitches or rows being referenced. Do not infer that a turning chain counts as a stitch unless the pattern says so.

For a multi-piece project, label pieces and note which side is the right side. The written instructions may use “right side” and “wrong side” to describe the fabric face, not the direction in which your hook moves. Keep the notes beside the row where they apply.

Use counts to diagnose a mismatch

At the end of a row, compare your work with the stated count and the pattern’s counting convention. If you are short, inspect skipped stitches, missed increases, and repeat boundaries. If you have too many, check whether a chain or stitch was worked twice. A mismatch does not always mean the last stitch is wrong; trace the row from its start and locate the first place the sequence diverges.

Write down the row number and any special change after finishing it. This is especially useful when the pattern alternates plain rows with shaping or repeats.

Pattern-specific mistakes

  • Starting before reading the notes: this can lead to the wrong terminology or an overlooked gauge instruction. Read the key and materials first; restart early if a basic convention was misunderstood.
  • Repeating the whole line instead of the marked group: brackets and parentheses may have different jobs. Follow the pattern’s own punctuation explanation and count completed repeats.
  • Counting spaces as stitches: chain spaces may be referenced separately from stitch tops. Compare the pattern’s wording and stated total before adding a stitch.

Practical reading habits

  • Use a removable marker or ruler to keep your place on a printed page.
  • Highlight only the row you are working, then move the mark when the row is complete.
  • Write the finished stitch count beside the row rather than relying on memory.
