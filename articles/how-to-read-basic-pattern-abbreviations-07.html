How to Read Basic Crochet Pattern Abbreviations

Decode common crochet abbreviations by checking the pattern’s stitch key and terminology first.

By Ariane Moreira

Abbreviations save space, but they are not a universal language. A pattern’s notes take precedence, and the same letters can mean different stitches across regions.

Before you begin

Read the materials, special-stitch notes, and abbreviations before starting. Confirm whether the pattern uses US terms; US “dc” differs from UK “dc.”

Step-by-step instructions

  1. Find the stitch key and note unfamiliar terms before beginning the row.
  2. Separate stitch names from instructions: “sc” names a US single crochet, while “ch” tells you to chain.
  3. Read repeat punctuation as part of the instruction. Brackets and asterisks can be defined differently by different patterns.
  4. Work one row slowly and compare its stitch count with the stated total.

What to check

Pattern notes outrank memory

If an abbreviation is missing, do not guess from another pattern. Check the designer’s notes or publisher and keep a short list of definitions beside your work.

Frequently asked questions

Does “dc” always mean double crochet?

No. In US terminology it means double crochet; in UK terminology it means treble crochet.

What does an asterisk mean?

It marks a repeat in many patterns, but the repeat’s endpoint and number of times vary. Follow the exact wording.

What does “rep” mean?

It usually means “repeat,” but the pattern defines what to repeat and how many times. Read the full instruction and punctuation.

Read a complete instruction before making the first stitch

Abbreviations are easier to understand in a full line than as isolated letters. Find the verb or stitch name, note the location such as “next stitch” or “space,” and then follow any repeat punctuation. Parentheses can group stitches worked into one place; brackets or asterisks may mark repeats, but each designer can define the notation in the pattern notes. Never assume a symbol’s repeat count from another pattern. Read the line through once, then identify where the repeat starts and ends.

Check the terms and repeat count together

A row can use familiar abbreviations but still be easy to misread if the terminology is different. Confirm US or UK terms first, especially for “dc” and “tr.” Then separate the stitch instructions from the repeat: count how many times the bracketed or starred section is worked and whether the pattern says to repeat to the end or a stated number of times. If the row total is provided, use it as a check after working the row. If the total conflicts with your interpretation, stop and review the key instead of changing stitches to force a match.

Use the stitch key to decode shorthand

A key may define common terms such as “sc” for US single crochet, “ch” for chain, and “sk” for skip. Other terms, such as an increase, decrease, or special stitch, can have pattern-specific instructions. A line that says to work a stitch in each of the next three stitches tells you where the stitches go; a separate repeat marker tells you which portion to do again. Keep the written line intact while you decode it, because punctuation can affect the result. If the key does not explain a term, check the pattern publisher’s notes before choosing a substitute. This check keeps a familiar abbreviation from replacing the pattern’s own definition.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

  • Assuming abbreviations are universal. For example, US and UK patterns use “dc” for different stitches. Check the terminology before beginning.
  • Stopping a repeat at the wrong point. Asterisks and brackets can mark different sections. Follow that pattern’s exact start, end, and repeat count.
  • Treating every abbreviation as a stitch name. Some abbreviations name stitches; others describe actions such as chain, skip, or repeat. Read them in context.

Tips that make it easier

  • Keep a short legend beside the pattern for terms you need to look up more than once.
  • Mark repeat boundaries before making the first stitch in a row.
  • Check the publisher’s key when its shorthand is missing from a general abbreviation list.

A practical next step

Keep a short legend beside the pattern for terms you need to look up more than once. Then check your result against the pattern before continuing.
